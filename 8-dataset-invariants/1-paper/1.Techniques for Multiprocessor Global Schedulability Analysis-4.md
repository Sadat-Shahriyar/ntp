### 4. Partitioned vs Global FJP scheduling

**Lemma 1 (statement and proof sketch — exact):**

> **Lemma 1** There are task systems that are schedulable using global FJP algorithms that partitioned FJP algorithms cannot schedule.
> **Proof Sketch:** Here’s an example task system, on 2 processors:
>
> $\tau_1 = (2, 2, 3), \tau_2 = (3, 3, 4), \tau_3 = (5, 12, 12)$
>
> Clearly, this system cannot be scheduled using partitioning, since $u_1 + u_2, u_1 + u_3$, and $u_2 + u_3$ are each $> 1$. To show that a global FJP algorithm can schedule this task system, consider any FJP algorithm which assigns lowest priority to $\tau_3$’s jobs. Since there are two processors, $\tau_1$ and $\tau_2$’s jobs are guaranteed to meet all deadlines. Now observe that each job of $\tau_3$ needs to execute for 5 time-slots over an interval of 12 slots1. Now over any 12 contiguous slots, $\tau_1$ may execute during at most 8 slots, thereby leaving at least 4 slots for $\tau_3$’s job upon one processor.
>
> • If $\tau_1$ executes for fewer than 8 slots between a job of $\tau_3$’s scheduling window, then it is straightforward to see that the job of $\tau_3$ completes execution by its deadline, since no more than one other job (of $\tau_2$) will be active during the at least 5 time units of this job’s scheduling window.
> • If $\tau_1$’s jobs execute for exactly 8 slots during the scheduling window of one of $\tau_3$’s jobs, it is easily seen that $\tau_1$’s jobs must be arriving exactly 3 time-units apart. Hence, it is not possible that $\tau_3$’s jobs execute in parallel with $\tau_1$’s jobs during all 8 of the time-slots during which $\tau_1$’s jobs execute within this scheduling window. Therefore there are at least 5 slots within this scheduling window during which one of both processors is idle after both $\tau_1$ and $\tau_2$ have been scheduled. 

**Lemma 2 (statement and proof sketch — exact):**

> **Lemma 2** There are task systems that are schedulable using partitioned FJP algorithms that global FJP algorithms cannot schedule.
> **Proof Sketch:** Here’s an example task system, on 2 processors:
>
> $\tau_1 = (2, 2, 3), \tau_2 = (3, 3, 4), \tau_3 = (4, 12, 12), \tau_4 = (3, 12, 12)$
>
> This task system may be partitioned by assigning $\tau_1$ and $\tau_3$ to one processor and the other two tasks to the remaining processor, and scheduling each processor using uniprocessor EDF.
> To show that no global FJP priority assignment scheme can meet all deadlines, consider the synchronous arrival sequence over the interval $[0, 12)$. It may be verified that whichever of $\tau_3$’s and $\tau_4$’s job has lower priority ends up missing its deadline while one processor goes idle over $[11, 12)$. 

**Theorem 1 (exact):**

> **Theorem 1** Global and partitioned FJP scheduling are incomparable.

### 5. Prior global-EDF results

**Density test (Eq. (1) — exact):**
$$
\delta_{\mathrm{sum}}(\tau) \le m - (m-1),\delta_{\max}(\tau)\ . \tag{1}

### 6. An improved schedulability algorithm (new constraints/tests)

#### Notation setup for the failure case (used by all formulas below)

Let a job of task $\tau_k$ **first** miss its deadline at time $t_d$; let its arrival be $t_a=t_d-D_k$; let $t_0$ be the latest instant $\le t_a$ when at least one processor is idle; define $A_k \stackrel{\text{def}}{=} t_a-t_0$. Let $\Gamma_k$ be any collection of intervals of total length $(D_k-C_k)$ inside $[t_a,t_d)$ where all $m$ processors execute jobs **other than** $\tau_k$’s job.  

---

#### Necessary total-work condition for a deadline miss (Eq. (2) — exact)

$$
\sum_{\tau_i\in\tau} I(\tau_i) ;>; m \times (A_k + D_k - C_k)\ ; \tag{2}
$$

> “…all $m$ processors are, by definition, completely busy executing this work over the $A_k$ time units in $[t_0, t_a)$, as well as the intervals in $\Gamma_k$ of total length $(D_k - C_k)$.”  

**Symbols explained**
$I(\tau_i)$: total contribution (work) by task $\tau_i$ within $[t_0,t_a)\ \cup\ \Gamma_k$ in the EDF schedule under consideration. $m$: processors. $A_k, D_k, C_k$: as above.

---

#### Upper bounds on per-task work when there is **no carry-in** (Eq. (3) — exact)

$$
I_1(\tau_i) \stackrel{\mathrm{def}}{=}
\begin{cases}
\min!\bigl(\mathrm{DBF}(\tau_i, A_k + D_k),\ A_k + D_k - C_k\bigr) & \text{if } i \ne k[2pt]
\min!\bigl(\mathrm{DBF}(\tau_i, A_k + D_k) - C_k,\ A_k\bigr) & \text{if } i = k
\end{cases} \tag{3}
$$
(Arrivals and deadlines within $[t_0,t_d)$; cannot exceed interval length; subtract $C_k$ for the target job when $i=k$.)  

**Symbols explained**
$I_1(\tau_i)$: bound on work of $\tau_i$ with **no carry-in**. $\mathrm{DBF}(\tau_i,t)$: demand bound function (defined earlier). $A_k,D_k,C_k$ as above.

---

#### Carry-in workload abstraction $\mathrm{DBF}'$ (Eq. (4) — exact)

Let $\mathrm{DBF}'(\tau_i,t)$ denote the work over any length-$t$ interval **ending at a job deadline** of $\tau_i$ if each job executes in the last $C_i$ time units before its deadline:
$$
\mathrm{DBF}'(\tau_i, t) \stackrel{\mathrm{def}}{=}
\left\lfloor \frac{t}{T_i} \right\rfloor \times C_i ;+; \min!\bigl(C_i,\ t \bmod T_i\bigr).\tag{4}
$$
(There are $\lfloor t/T_i\rfloor$ complete jobs plus at most $C_i$ from the carry-in window of the straddling job.) 

**Symbols explained**
$\lfloor\cdot\rfloor$: floor. $t\bmod T_i$: remainder. $C_i,T_i$ as above.

---

#### Upper bounds on per-task work when there **is carry-in** (Eq. (5) — exact)

$$
I_2(\tau_i) \stackrel{\mathrm{def}}{=}
\begin{cases}
\min!\bigl(\mathrm{DBF}'(\tau_i, A_k + D_k),\ A_k + D_k - C_k\bigr) & \text{if } i \ne k[2pt]
\min!\bigl(\mathrm{DBF}'(\tau_i, A_k + D_k) - C_k,\ A_k\bigr) & \text{if } i = k
\end{cases} \tag{5}
$$


**Symbols explained**
$I_2(\tau_i)$: bound on work of $\tau_i$ **with** carry-in (uses $\mathrm{DBF}'$). Other symbols as above.

#### Difference term (Eq. (6) — exact)

$$
\mathrm{IDIFF}(\tau_i) \stackrel{\mathrm{def}}{=} I_2(\tau_i) - I_1(\tau_i). \tag{6}
$$


**Symbols explained**
$\mathrm{IDIFF}$: additional work due to carry-in for $\tau_i$.

#### Aggregated necessary condition rewritten (Eq. (7) — exact)

Since **at most $(m-1)$ tasks** can be active at $t_0$, only $(m-1)$ tasks may contribute with $I_2(\cdot)$; the rest contribute with $I_1(\cdot)$:
$$
\sum_{\tau_i\in\tau} I_1(\tau_i) ;+; \sum_{\text{the $(m-1)$ largest}} \mathrm{IDIFF}(\tau_i);>; m(A_k + D_k - C_k). \tag{7}
$$


**Symbols explained**
The second sum selects the $(m-1)$ largest $\mathrm{IDIFF}(\tau_i)$ values.

#### **Main schedulability test** (Theorem 2 / Condition (8) — exact)

> **Theorem 2** Task system $\tau$ is EDF-schedulable upon $m$ unit-capacity processors **if for all** tasks $\tau_k \in \tau$ and **all** $A_k \ge 0$,
> $$
> \left(\sum_{\tau_i\in\tau} I_1(\tau_i) ;+; \sum_{\text{the $(m-1)$ largest}} \mathrm{IDIFF}(\tau_i)\right)
> ;\le; m(A_k + D_k - C_k) \tag{8}
> $$
> where $I_1(\tau_i)$ and $\mathrm{IDIFF}(\tau_i)$ are as defined in Equations 3 and 6 respectively. 

**Symbols explained**
All previously defined: $I_1,I_2,\mathrm{IDIFF},A_k,C_k,D_k,m$.

#### Uniprocessor exactness (Corollary 1 — exact + proof sketch)

> **Corollary 1** The EDF schedulability test of Theorem 2 is a generalization of the exact uniprocessor EDF schedulability test of [9].
> **Proof Sketch:** For $m = 1$, there are $(m − 1) = 0$ tasks that are active at time-instant $t_0$; i.e., $t_0$ is the classical “idle instant” of uniprocessor real-time scheduling theory. By adding $C_k$ to both the LHS and the RHS of Condition 8, it can be shown that the LHS reduces to the sum of the demand bound functions of all tasks over an interval of size $A_k+D_k$, and the RHS reduces to the interval length. Hence, when $m = 1$ Condition 8 is asserting that the cumulative processor demand over all intervals must not exceed the interval length, which is exactly what the uniprocessor EDF schedulability test of [9] checks.

#### Finite search bound for $A_k$ (Theorem 3 / Eq. (9) — exact + proof)

> **Theorem 3** If Condition 8 is to be violated for any $A_k$, then it is violated for some $A_k$ satisfying the condition below:
> $$
> A_k ;\le; \frac{,C_\Sigma - D_k,(m - U(\tau)) + \sum_i (T_i - D_i)U_i + mC_k,}{,m - U(\tau),} \tag{9}
> $$
> where $C_\Sigma$ denotes the sum of the $(m − 1)$ largest $C_i$’s.
> **Proof:** *It is easily seen that $I_1(\tau_i) \le \mathrm{DBF}(\tau_i, A_k + D_k)$, and $I_2(\tau_i) \le \mathrm{DBF}(\tau_i, A_k + D_k) + C_i$. From this, it can be shown that the LHS of Condition 8 is $\le C_\Sigma + \sum_{\tau_i\in\tau} \mathrm{DBF}(\tau_i, A_k + D_k)$. For this to exceed the RHS of Condition 8, it is necessary that*
> $$
> C_\Sigma + \mathrm{DBF}(\tau, A_k + D_k) > m(A_k + D_k - C_k)
> $$
> *$\Rightarrow$ (bounding DBF using the technique of [9])*
> $$
> C_\Sigma + (A_k + D_k)U(\tau) + \sum_i (T_i - D_i)U_i > m(A_k + D_k - C_k)
> $$
> *$\equiv\ C_\Sigma + D_kU(\tau) + \sum_i (T_i - D_i)U_i - m(D_k - C_k) > A_k(m - U(\tau))$*
>
> *$\equiv\ A_k \le \dfrac{,C_\Sigma - D_k(m - U(\tau)) + \sum_i (T_i - D_i)U_i + mC_k,}{,m - U(\tau),}$ which is as claimed in the theorem.*
> *It can also be shown that Condition 8 need only be tested at those values of $A_k$ at which $\mathrm{DBF}(\tau_i, A_k + D_k)$ changes for some $\tau_i$. Corollary 2 follows.* 

**Corollary 2 (exact):**

> **Corollary 2** The condition in Theorem 2 above can be tested in time pseudo-polynomial in the task parameters, for all task systems $\tau$ for which $U(\tau)$ is bounded by a constant strictly less than the number of processors $m$. 

**Symbols explained (for Theorem 3 / Cor. 2)**
$U(\tau)=\sum_i U_i$ total utilization; $U_i=u_i=C_i/T_i$; $C_\Sigma$ = sum of the $(m-1)$ largest $C_i$’s; $T_i,D_i,C_i,m$ as before. The bound (9) *restricts* the values of $A_k$ one must check in Theorem 2, making the test pseudo-polynomial.
