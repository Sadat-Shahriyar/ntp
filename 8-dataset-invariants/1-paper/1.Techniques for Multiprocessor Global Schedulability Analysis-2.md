## 1) Modeling Assumptions (as background for the analysis)

### Workload model (sporadic tasks)

A sporadic task is $\tau_i=(C_i,D_i,T_i)$ with worst-case execution $C_i$, relative deadline $D_i$, and minimum inter-arrival time/period $T_i$. Jobs of $\tau_i$ have execution demand $C_i$ and deadlines $D_i$ after arrival; the interval of length $D_i$ from a job’s arrival to its deadline is its “scheduling window.” The paper considers constrained- ($D_i\le T_i$) and implicit-deadline ($D_i=T_i$) sporadic systems, with fully preemptive execution (no preemption overhead). Let $\tau={\tau_1,\ldots,\tau_n}$.   

Useful quantities (used throughout the tests):

* **Utilization:** $u_i=C_i/T_i$, $u_{\text{sum}}(\tau)=\sum_{\tau_i\in\tau}u_i$, $u_{\max}(\tau)=\max_{\tau_i\in\tau}u_i$. 
* **Density:** $\delta_i=C_i/D_i$, $\delta_{\text{sum}}(\tau)=\sum_{\tau_i\in\tau}\delta_i$, $\delta_{\max}(\tau)=\max_{\tau_i\in\tau}\delta_i$. 
* **Demand Bound Function (DBF):**
  $$
  \operatorname{DBF}(\tau_i,t)=\max!\left(0,\left(\left\lfloor\frac{t-D_i}{T_i}\right\rfloor+1\right)C_i\right).
  $$

* **LOAD parameter:** $\displaystyle \operatorname{LOAD}(\tau)=\max_{t>0}\frac{\sum_{\tau_i\in\tau}\operatorname{DBF}(\tau_i,t)}{t}$. 

### Resource model

$m$ **identical** unit-capacity processors (“upon $m$ unit-capacity processors” appears repeatedly in the tests). The system is fully preemptive.  

### Scheduler

The paper focuses on **global EDF** (a dynamic-priority, fixed-job-priority class instantiation), where at each instant, jobs are prioritized by earliest deadline; under “global” scheduling, jobs may migrate across processors.  

### Analysis framework

Schedulability = meeting all deadlines under all legal job-arrival sequences. The paper develops sufficient schedulability tests using DBF-based bounds, carry-in accounting, and a new condition that strictly generalizes the exact uniprocessor EDF test; it also discusses prior density/[BAK]/[BCL] tests.

> Example 1 Consider the task system comprised of the three tasks $\tau_1=(1,1,2)$, $\tau_2=(1,1,3)$, and $\tau_3=(5,6,6)$, executing upon two unit-capacity processors. ## 3) Partitioned vs Global FJP scheduling (Section 4)

### **Lemma 1** (statement and proof sketch — exact text)

> Lemma 1 There are task systems that are schedulable using global FJP algorithms that partitioned FJP algorithms cannot schedule.
> Proof Sketch: Here’s an example task system, on 2 processors:
>
> $\tau_1=(2,2,3),\ \tau_2=(3,3,4),\ \tau_3=(5,12,12)$
>
> Clearly, this system cannot be scheduled using partitioning, since $u_1+u_2$, $u_1+u_3$, and $u_2+u_3$ are each $>1$. To show that a global FJP algorithm can schedule this task system, consider any FJP algorithm which assigns lowest priority to $\tau_3$’s jobs. Since there are two processors, $\tau_1$ and $\tau_2$’s jobs are guaranteed to meet all deadlines. Now observe that each job of $\tau_3$ needs to execute for 5 time-slots over an interval of 12 slots$^1$. Now over any 12 contiguous slots, $\tau_1$ may execute during at most 8 slots, thereby leaving at least 4 slots for $\tau_3$’s job upon one processor.
> • If $\tau_1$ executes for fewer than 8 slots between a job of $\tau_3$’s scheduling window, then it is straightforward to see that the job of $\tau_3$ completes execution by its deadline, since no more than one other job (of $\tau_2$) will be active during the at least 5 time units of this job’s scheduling window.
> • If $\tau_1$’s jobs execute for exactly 8 slots during the scheduling window of one of $\tau_3$’s jobs, it is easily seen that $\tau_1$’s jobs must be arriving exactly 3 time-units apart. Hence, it is not possible that $\tau_3$’s jobs execute in parallel with $\tau_1$’s jobs during all 8 of the time-slots during which $\tau_1$’s jobs execute within this scheduling window. Therefore there are at least 5 slots within this scheduling window during which one of both processors is idle after both $\tau_1$ and $\tau_2$ have been scheduled.  

### **Lemma 2** (statement and proof sketch — exact text)

> Lemma 2 There are task systems that are schedulable using partitioned FJP algorithms that global FJP algorithms cannot schedule.
> Proof Sketch: Here’s an example task system, on 2 processors:
>
> $\tau_1=(2,2,3),\ \tau_2=(3,3,4),\ \tau_3=(4,12,12),\ \tau_4=(3,12,12)$
>
> This task system may be partitioned by assigning $\tau_1$ and $\tau_3$ to one processor and the other two tasks to the remaining processor, and scheduling each processor using uniprocessor EDF.
>
> To show that no global FJP priority assignment scheme can meet all deadlines, consider the synchronous arrival sequence over the interval $[0,12)$. It may be verified that whichever of $\tau_3$’s and $\tau_4$’s job has lower priority ends up missing its deadline while one processor goes idle over $[11,12)$. 

### **Theorem 1** (statement — exact text)

> Theorem 1 Global and partitioned FJP scheduling are incomparable.

## 4) Global EDF schedulability — Prior results (Section 5)

### **Density test** (sufficient condition; exact equation (1))

$$
\delta_{\text{sum}}(\tau)\ \le\ m - (m-1),\delta_{\max}(\tau),. \tag{1}
$$
This is a sufficient global-EDF schedulability condition for constrained-deadline sporadic systems on $m$ unit-capacity processors (derived from the implicit-deadline case).  

**Notation:**
$\delta_{\text{sum}}(\tau)=\sum_i C_i/D_i$, $\delta_{\max}(\tau)=\max_i(C_i/D_i)$, $m$ = number of processors. (Definitions earlier.) 

### **[BAK] and [BCL] tests** (strategy summary)

Both assume a first missed deadline of a job of $\tau_k$ at time $t_d$, analyze the interval $[t_o,t_d)$ (with different choices of $t_o$), and bound the required executed work (including carry-in) to derive sufficient conditions.

## 5) An improved global EDF schedulability algorithm (Section 6)

**Setup and notation** (used to derive the new condition):

* Consider a legal job sequence where $\tau_k$’s job is **first** to miss a deadline at $t_d$, having arrived at $t_a=t_d-D_k$. Define $t_o$ as the **latest time $\le t_a$** when some processor is idle; let $A_k\stackrel{\text{def}}{=}t_a-t_o$. 
* Let $\Gamma_k$ be any set of intervals totaling length $(D_k-C_k)$ inside $[t_a,t_d)$ during which **all $m$ processors** execute jobs other than $\tau_k$’s job. 
* For each $\tau_i$, let $I(\tau_i)$ be its contribution to the executed work over $[t_o,t_a)\ \cup\ \Gamma_k$. A **necessary** condition for the miss is:
  $$
  \sum_{\tau_i\in\tau} I(\tau_i)\ >\ m\cdot(A_k + D_k - C_k),.\tag{2}
  $$
  (All $m$ processors are fully busy over $[t_o,t_a)$ of length $A_k$ and during $\Gamma_k$ of length $D_k-C_k$.)  

We now upper-bound $I(\tau_i)$ with/without carry-in:

* **No carry-in ($I_1$):**
  $$
  I_1(\tau_i)\stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\begin{cases}
  \min\bigl(\operatorname{DBF}(\tau_i,A_k+D_k),,A_k+D_k-C_k\bigr)&\text{if }i\ne k
  \min\bigl(\operatorname{DBF}(\tau_i,A_k+D_k)-C_k,,A_k\bigr)&\text{if }i=k
  \end{cases}\tag{3}
  $$
  (From DBF over $[t_o,t_d)$ and interval length limits.) 

* **Carry-in definition $\operatorname{DBF}'$ and carry-in bound ($I_2$):**
  If a job of $\tau_i$ has deadline at the interval end and each job executes just before its deadline, the **carry-in aware** demand over any interval of length $t$ is
  $$
  \operatorname{DBF}'(\tau_i,t)\stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\left\lfloor \frac{t}{T_i}\right\rfloor C_i+\min!\bigl(C_i,\ t\bmod T_i\bigr).\tag{4}
  $$
  Then
  $$
  I_2(\tau_i)\stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\begin{cases}
  \min\bigl(\operatorname{DBF}'(\tau_i,A_k+D_k),,A_k+D_k-C_k\bigr)&\text{if }i\ne k
  \min\bigl(\operatorname{DBF}'(\tau_i,A_k+D_k)-C_k,,A_k\bigr)&\text{if }i=k
  \end{cases}\tag{5}
  $$
      

* **Difference term:** $\mathrm{IDIFF}(\tau_i)\stackrel{\text{def}}{=}I_2(\tau_i)-I_1(\tau_i)$. By definition of $t_o$, at most $(m-1)$ tasks are active at $t_o$ (hence at most $(m-1)$ carry-in contributors). Rewriting (2):
  $$
  \sum_{\tau_i\in\tau} I_1(\tau_i)+ \sum_{\text{the $(m-1)$ largest}} \mathrm{IDIFF}(\tau_i)\ >\ m(A_k+D_k-C_k),.\tag{7}
  $$
   

### **Theorem 2** (new sufficient schedulability test — exact statement and condition (8))

> Theorem 2 Task system $\tau$ is EDF-schedulable upon $m$ unit-capacity processors if for all tasks $\tau_k\in\tau$ and all $A_k\ge 0,$
> $$
> \left(\sum_{\tau_i\in\tau} I_1(\tau_i)\ +\ \sum_{\text{the $(m-1)$ largest}} \mathrm{IDIFF}(\tau_i)\right)\ \le\ m(A_k+D_k-C_k),.\tag{8}
> $$
> where $I_1(\tau_i)$ and $\mathrm{IDIFF}(\tau_i)$ are as defined in Equations 3 and 6 respectively. 

**Notation used in (2)–(8):**

* $m$ = processors; $\tau$ = task set; $\tau_i$ = task; $C_i,D_i,T_i$ as above.
* $\operatorname{DBF},\operatorname{DBF}'$ as in (3)–(4).
* $t_d$ = missed-deadline time; $t_a=t_d-D_k$; $t_o$ = latest idle time $\le t_a$; $A_k=t_a-t_o$; $\Gamma_k$ = intervals totaling $D_k-C_k$ where all $m$ CPUs run jobs other than $\tau_k$’s.
* $I(\tau_i)$ = executed work from $\tau_i$ over $[t_o,t_a)\cup\Gamma_k$; $I_1,I_2$ = bounds without/with carry-in; $\mathrm{IDIFF}=I_2-I_1$.   

### **Corollary 1** (uniprocessor exactness — exact text)

> Corollary 1 The EDF schedulability test of Theorem 2 is a generalization of the exact uniprocessor EDF schedulability test of [9].
> Proof Sketch: For $m=1$, there are $(m-1)=0$ tasks that are active at time-instant $t_o$; i.e., $t_o$ is the classical “idle instant” of uniprocessor real-time scheduling theory. By adding $C_k$ to both the LHS and the RHS of Condition 8, it can be shown that the LHS reduces to the sum of the demand bound functions of all tasks over an interval of size $A_k+D_k$, and the RHS reduces to the interval length. Hence, when $m=1$ Condition 8 is asserting that the cumulative processor demand over all intervals must not exceed the interval length, which is exactly what the uniprocessor EDF schedulability test of [9] checks.  

### **Run-time and bounding the search over $A_k$ (Section 6.1)**

* Evaluating Condition (8) is $O(n)$ for fixed $(\tau_k,A_k)$; selecting the $(m-1)$ largest $\mathrm{IDIFF}$ is linear-time selectable. 

* **Theorem 3** (bound on $A_k$ values to check — exact statement and equation (9))
  $$
  \text{If Condition 8 is violated for any }A_k,\text{ then it is violated for some }A_k\text{ s.t.}
  $$
  $$
  A_k\ \le\ \frac{C_\Sigma - D_k,(m-U(\tau)) + \sum_i (T_i-D_i)U_i + mC_k}{,m-U(\tau),},.\tag{9}
  $$
  where $C_\Sigma$ is the sum of the $(m-1)$ largest $C_i$’s. 

  **Proof (excerpted exactly where shown):**
  “It is easily seen that $I_1(\tau_i) \le \operatorname{DBF}(\tau_i,A_k+D_k)$, and $I_2(\tau_i)\le \operatorname{DBF}(\tau_i,A_k+D_k)+C_i$. From this, it can be shown that the LHS of Condition 8 is $\le C_\Sigma + \sum_{\tau_i\in\tau}\operatorname{DBF}(\tau_i, A_k+D_k)$. For this to exceed the RHS of Condition 8, it is necessary that
  $$
  C_\Sigma + \operatorname{DBF}(\tau, A_k+D_k) > m(A_k + D_k - C_k)
  $$
  $\Rightarrow$ (bounding DBF using the technique of [9])
  $$
  C_\Sigma + (A_k + D_k)U(\tau) + \sum_i (T_i - D_i)U_i > m(A_k + D_k - C_k)
  $$
  $\equiv\ C_\Sigma + D_k U(\tau) + \sum_i (T_i - D_i) U_i - m(D_k - C_k) > A_k(m - U(\tau))
  $$
  $\equiv\ A_k \le \frac{C_\Sigma - D_k(m - U(\tau)) + \sum_i (T_i - D_i)U_i + mC_k}{m - U(\tau)}
  $$
  which is as claimed in the theorem.” 

* **Corollary 2** (pseudo-polynomial time — exact text)

  > Corollary 2 The condition in Theorem 2 above can be tested in time pseudo-polynomial in the task parameters, for all task systems $\tau$ for which $U(\tau)$ is bounded by a constant strictly less than the number of processors $m$. 

**Notation used in (9):**
$U_i=u_i=C_i/T_i$, $U(\tau)=\sum_i U_i$, $C_\Sigma=\sum$ of $(m-1)$ largest $C_i$, and the rest as above.

## 6) Additional background conditions (necessary but not sufficient)

* **LOAD necessary condition for global EDF:** $\operatorname{LOAD}(\tau)\le m$ is **necessary** (but not sufficient) for EDF schedulability on $m$ processors; Example 1 shows insufficiency. (The paper stresses this nuance.)

## 7) Prior density test for implicit deadlines (context)

For implicit-deadline (Liu & Layland) systems:
$$
u_{\text{sum}}(\tau)\ \le\ m - (m-1),u_{\max}(\tau)
$$
is a sufficient global-EDF condition; the constrained-deadline density form (1) generalizes it.

# Symbol Reference (quick recap for all constraints)

* $m$: number of identical unit-capacity processors. 
* $\tau,\tau_i$: task set and $i$-th sporadic task. 
* $C_i,D_i,T_i$: WCET, relative deadline, period/min. inter-arrival time. 
* $u_i=C_i/T_i$, $U(\tau)=\sum_i u_i$, $u_{\max}$; $\delta_i=C_i/D_i$, $\delta_{\text{sum}}$, $\delta_{\max}$.  
* $\operatorname{DBF}(\tau_i,t)$: standard demand bound function. 
* $\operatorname{DBF}'(\tau_i,t)$: carry-in aware demand (Eq. 4). 
* $t_d,t_a,t_o$: miss time, arrival time, latest prior idle time; $A_k=t_a-t_o$. 
* $\Gamma_k$: intervals in $[t_a,t_d)$ of total length $D_k-C_k$ where all $m$ CPUs run jobs $\ne \tau_k$. 
* $I(\tau_i)$, $I_1$, $I_2$, $\mathrm{IDIFF}$: executed-work contribution and its bounds/difference (Eqs. 3–6).  

---

## What to use as the **schedulability constraints/invariants** in practice

* **Density test (1)** — quick sufficient check for constrained deadlines. 
* **Main sufficient test (8)** — *the* improved global-EDF condition; check for every $k$ and for all $A_k\ge 0$ (bounded by (9)); uses only $(m-1)$ carry-in contributors.  
* **Uniprocessor specialization** — matches the exact EDF test when $m=1$ (Cor. 1). 
* **Runtime** — pseudo-polynomial when $U(\tau)<m$ (Cor. 2) and $A_k$ search bounded by (9).
